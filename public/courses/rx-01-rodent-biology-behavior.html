By the end of this course you will be able to:

Explain how rodent senses, wall-hugging and spatial memory decide where devices go and why they stay put

Why this course matters

The animal decides the job. Not the product.

Every Rodent Shield decision, from where you inspect to what you seal to how long you wait, comes from how these animals live. Get the biology wrong and the job fails, however hard you work.

A Clements Rodent Shield Certification tells customers their technician went through structured training above state licensing. This course is where that starts.

Fire, damage and contamination

Constantly growing teeth mean constant gnawing: wiring (a documented fire cause), A/C lines, insulation soaked with urine, food and surfaces contaminated with droppings.

Real disease risk, stated accurately

Salmonella, leptospirosis and rat-bite fever. In Florida, hantavirus is linked to native cotton rats and rice rats, not these three. Never sweep or vacuum droppings dry: wet them with disinfectant first (CDC). Field safety is RX-08.

Trust comes from explaining, not alarming

"Five mice today can be fifty by summer" is true and calm. A tech who can explain the animal sells the right fix and keeps the customer when traps are quiet for a week.

03 · Teeth and gnawing

A rat's teeth grow about 5 inches a year

UF/IFAS puts rat incisor growth at 4½ to 5½ inches a year. The animal has to gnaw every day to keep its teeth short and sharp, so it chews whether it is hungry or not.

What that means on site

Fresh gnawing on wiring, PVC, door sweeps, A/C line insulation, fascia and fruit. Rat gnaw marks are wider and deeper than mouse marks; fine, shallow scratches point to mice.

Fresh or old?

Fresh: pale wood, sharp edges, chips nearby. Old: darkened, weathered, dust in the grooves. Fresh gnawing at a gap means that entry is active.

Why foam is not exclusion: a rodent will chew through foam, caulk, rubber and wood. Rodent Shield seals with cement and metal for exactly this reason (materials in RX-04).

02 · What can it get through?

If the skull fits, the body follows

Mouse ¼ in: CDC ("the width of a pencil"). Rat ½ in, especially young rats: UC IPM and UF/IFAS. Household comparisons mislead: a dime is about ¾ in across, three times what a mouse needs.

03 · Food, water and range

How far from the nest? It depends who's eating.

Use the range on every inspection. Mouse droppings in a drawer: the nest is close, usually within 10–30 ft. Roof rat in the attic: the food may be the neighbor's mango tree.

05 · Talking biology with customers

Grounded in biology, never scare tactics

"Why no catches yet?"

"Rats are wary of anything new on their path. We let them get used to the devices first, then the catches come. Moving them now would start the clock over."

"How did they get in?"

"Roof rats are climbers. A rat can squeeze through a gap about half an inch wide, so we check the roofline, the vents and where that palm touches the roof."

"Will they come back?"

"If we only trap, more can move in from the neighborhood. If we seal the entry points and trap what's inside, the house stays closed. That's why we seal first."

Pricing and tier questions go to the service advisor or branch manager. Describe what you found and why it matters; never quote a price or promise a result. Tiers, warranty and renewals are covered in CW-05 and RX-06.
Never say
  • "You'll get hantavirus" or any other scare line
  • "They'll never come back," or any promise or price
  • That a bait or product is safe, harmless or non-toxic
