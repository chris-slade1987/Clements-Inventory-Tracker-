Why PPE matters

PPE is your last line of defense.

Safe handling and good application technique come first. PPE is what protects you when a splash, a drift or a leak gets past them. Years on the job don't change the chemistry: one splash or one forgotten glove can do serious harm.

The label is the law

The PPE listed on the label is the legal minimum under federal law (FIFRA) and Florida Chapter 482, enforced by FDACS. Wearing less is a violation, even for one quick job.

OSHA covers the workplace

OSHA requires Clements to assess job hazards, provide the right PPE, and train you to use it (29 CFR 1910.132). Respirators fall under their own standard (1910.134).

You make it work

The best gear in the truck protects no one. It only works if you wear it, wear it correctly, and keep it in good condition.

The risk

Four ways a pesticide gets into your body

Every piece of PPE blocks one or more of these routes. Select each to see what it looks like on the job.

Skin is the most common route for applicators, and your hands and forearms take most of it, especially when mixing and loading concentrate.
Reading the label

Find the PPE statement before you mix anything.

It sits under Precautionary Statements, usually titled "Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)." Read three things:

  1. 1Who it applies to. "Applicators and other handlers" means you. Some labels add extra items for mixers and loaders only.
  2. 2Each item listed. Every item is required, not suggested.
  3. 3The signal word. CAUTION, WARNING, then DANGER: the higher the hazard, the more PPE the label usually requires.
Example label · Precautionary statements

CAUTION

Hazards to Humans and Domestic Animals. Harmful if absorbed through skin. Causes moderate eye irritation. Avoid contact with skin, eyes or clothing.

Personal Protective Equipment (PPE). Applicators and other handlers must wear: long-sleeved shirt and long pants, shoes plus socks, and chemical-resistant gloves. Mixers and loaders must also wear protective eyewear.

Follow manufacturer's instructions for cleaning and maintaining PPE. If no such instructions for washables exist, use detergent and hot water. Keep and wash PPE separately from other laundry.

Gear up · every technician

The Clements base kit

Worn on every service, GHP and L&O alike, before the label adds anything. Select each item to put it on.

0 of 5 base items on. The respirator is added only when the label or the task calls for it.

Hands

Gloves: the right material, worn the right way

Use

Unlined chemical-resistant gloves: nitrile, neoprene or butyl. When a label lists glove materials it often specifies 14 mil or thicker. Thin disposable nitrile is for baiting and light work, not for handling concentrate unless the label allows it.

Never use

Cotton, leather, canvas or fabric-lined gloves. They soak up pesticide and hold it against your skin all day, which is worse than wearing nothing.

Sleeves and cuffs

Spraying at or below waist height: sleeves over the gloves, so liquid runs off. Spraying overhead: glove cuffs over the sleeves, folded back, so nothing runs down your arm.

Before you take reusable gloves off, wash the outside with soap and water. Then remove them, and wash your hands before you eat, drink, smoke, use your phone or use the restroom.
Eyes, feet and body

Match the protection to the splash risk

Eyes and face

Safety glasses with side shields for routine work. Splash goggles when pouring or mixing concentrate, or when the label says "goggles." A face shield adds face protection but is never a substitute for glasses or goggles underneath.

Feet

Slip-resistant, closed-toe footwear with socks, always. When the label says "chemical-resistant footwear," wear rubber boots, with pant legs outside the boots so spray can't run inside. Leather and canvas absorb pesticide.

Body

Long-sleeved shirt and long pants. A chemical-resistant apron for mixing, loading and cleaning equipment. Coveralls for attics, crawl spaces and heavy L&O spraying. A wide-brimmed hat helps keep drift and sun off your skin.

Practice · read it, then gear up

Select everything this job requires

Respirators

Know which respirator, and what it stops

Particulate (N95 or P100 filters)

Stops dusts, mists and particles: attic dusting, granular dust, rodent-contaminated areas. Does not stop vapors. An N95 alone will not protect you from solvent fumes.

Half-face with organic vapor cartridges

Stops pesticide vapors, usually paired with a particulate pre-filter for spray mists. Use it whenever the label calls for an organic vapor (OV) respirator.

NIOSH-approved only, and the type the label names. The approval is printed on the mask and cartridges.
Color tells you the cartridge. Black band = organic vapor. Magenta = P100 particulate.
Never share a respirator. Yours is fitted to your face.
Before you can wear one

Cleared, fitted, sealed

  1. 1Medical clearance before your first respirator use. Breathing through a filter puts extra load on your heart and lungs.
  2. 2Fit test before first use and every year after, on the exact make, model and size you'll wear.
  3. 3Clean-shaven where the mask seals. Beard stubble breaks the seal. If facial hair touches the sealing surface, you can't wear a tight-fitting respirator.
  4. 4User seal check every time you put it on. Try both checks on the right.

Positive: cover the exhalation valve with your palm and breathe out gently. The mask should bulge slightly with no air leaking at the edges.

Cartridges and filters

Vapor cartridges give no warning. Change them on schedule.

Organic vapor cartridges

They don't get harder to breathe through as they fill up. Follow the label and the manufacturer's change schedule. When neither gives one, replace them at the end of each day of use. If you smell or taste chemical, or feel irritation, leave the area and replace them immediately.

Particulate filters

These do load up. Replace them when breathing gets harder, or when they're wet, damaged or visibly dirty.

Cartridges keep absorbing from the air even when you're not wearing the mask. Store the respirator in a sealed bag between uses.
After each use, remove the cartridges, wash the facepiece with mild soap and water, rinse and air-dry before bagging.
Inspect before every use

Damaged PPE is no PPE

Gloves

Trap air inside and squeeze. Any leak, crack, swelling or stiffness: replace.

Respirator

Cracked facepiece, stretched straps, missing valve flaps, cartridges past schedule.

Eyewear

Scratched or cracked lenses, broken side shields, loose straps.

Boots, apron, coveralls

Cracks, tears, holes or soaked-in stains.

Putting it on: coveralls or apron, then boots, then respirator (with a seal check), then eyewear, then gloves last, so your gloves stay clean while you dress.
Practice · taking it off

Remove PPE without contaminating yourself

Most exposure from PPE happens when you take it off. Select the steps in the order you'd do them.

Cleaning and storage

Clean it every day. Keep it away from the chemicals.

Laundry

Wash work clothes and reusable coveralls separately from family laundry, in hot water with heavy-duty detergent. Then run an empty cycle to rinse the machine.

Soaked with concentrate?

Don't wash it. Clothing drenched with concentrate can't be decontaminated reliably. Bag it, tell your supervisor, and discard it. Disposable coveralls and gloves are single use.

Hard goods

Wash gloves, boots, goggles, face shields and the respirator facepiece with soap and water after each day of use. Air-dry before storing.

On the truck: keep clean PPE in a dedicated, closed container in the cab, never in the chemical compartment or next to pesticide containers.
Heat and sun break gear down. Never leave a respirator or goggles on the dashboard. Bring the respirator in at the end of the day.
Working in Florida heat

PPE traps heat. Plan for it. Don't take it off.

Heat exhaustion

Heavy sweating, cool clammy skin, headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness. Stop. Move to shade or the cab with the AC on, remove PPE safely, sip water. If it doesn't improve within an hour, get medical care.

Heat stroke · emergency

Confusion, slurred speech, fainting, seizures, very high body temperature. Call 911. Cool the person immediately with water and shade while you wait.

Drink water about every 15 to 20 minutes on hot days, even before you feel thirsty.
Schedule attics and heavy-PPE jobs early in the day when you can.
Never remove required PPE to cool off mid-application. Stop the job and take a break instead.
Part two

Emergency procedures

Even with PPE, accidents happen. The first minutes decide how serious an exposure becomes. Always check the label's First Aid section; it overrides general steps.

Emergency · 01 of 04

Skin and clothing

  1. 1Stop work. Remove contaminated clothing, gloves and boots immediately.
  2. 2Rinse skin with plenty of water for 15 to 20 minutes, then wash with soap and water. Use the water on the truck or the nearest hose.
  3. 3Follow the label's First Aid section and call Poison Control for treatment advice.
  4. 4Bag contaminated clothing in a sealed plastic bag. Don't take it into your house or wash it with other laundry.
Emergency · 02 of 04

Chemical in eyes

  1. 1Hold the eye open and rinse gently with clean water for 15 to 20 minutes.
  2. 2Contact lenses: remove them after the first 5 minutes, then keep rinsing.
  3. 3Do not rub your eyes.
  4. 4Get medical attention, even if it feels better. Take the label.

Fifteen minutes feels much longer than you expect. Keep rinsing the whole time.

Emergency · 03 of 04

Inhalation

  1. 1Get to fresh air right away. Help a coworker out only if you won't be exposed too.
  2. 2Loosen tight clothing and stay calm.
  3. 3Not breathing or struggling to breathe: call 911.
  4. 4Call Poison Control for treatment advice, then report it.
Emergency · 04 of 04

Ingestion

  1. 1Call Poison Control or a doctor immediately. Have the label in hand.
  2. 2Do not induce vomiting unless Poison Control or a doctor tells you to.
  3. 3Give water only if they instruct it and the person can swallow.
  4. 4Never give anything by mouth to an unconscious person. Call 911.
Emergency · 05 of 05

Who to call, and what to report

  1. 1Get care first: 911 for anything life-threatening, Poison Control for treatment advice.
  2. 2Tell your direct supervisor right away, then your branch manager.
  3. 3Complete an incident report the same day, even if you feel fine. Pesticide symptoms can show up hours later.
When you call, have the label or SDS ready: product name, EPA registration number, active ingredient, how the exposure happened and how long ago.
