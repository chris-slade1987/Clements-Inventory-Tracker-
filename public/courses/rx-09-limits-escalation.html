Why this course matters

Knowing the limit and calling it is part of being a professional.

"Trying to perform a proper exclusion through structural damage is how warranty failures happen."CLEMENTS RODENT CERTIFICATION TRAINING · WHAT'S NOT IN SCOPE

Rodent jobs are where techs get hurt

Roofs, ladders, 120°F attics, gnawed wiring and biological hazards. Falls are a leading cause of serious workplace injury. No exclusion job is worth that.

Work outside scope fails, and the law has limits

Sealing over rot fails in months. Roofing, structural and electrical repairs need a contractor's license. Bats and other wildlife are protected by state rules.

Customers trust a straight answer

"I want to do this right rather than fast" keeps a customer. A guess about price, coverage or their health can cost the company the account and its reputation.

01 · Your lane, and the three answers

What a certified rodent technician does

Inside your scope
  • Inspect, identify and document evidence and entry points (all openings ½ in and larger on Plus)
  • Place traps and tamper-resistant, anchored bait stations
  • Seal entry points with approved materials: cement first, ½-inch threshold
  • Clean up minor droppings in small, easy-to-reach areas (about 2 ft by 2 ft), wetted first
  • Explain findings and conducive conditions to the customer
  • Small "while I'm here" repairs that are within scope, noted in the service notes
Do it

Inside your scope, safe, and you've been trained for it. Do it to the Clements standard and document it.

Escalate

Clements has to decide: price, scope, safety, a dispute, a warranty claim, or anything you're unsure of. Your branch manager, the same day.

Refer

It belongs to someone else: the Clements wildlife team, a licensed roofer, contractor or electrician, or the customer's doctor or vet. Tell your branch manager too.

02 · What we never do

Eight hard stops, on every job, for every customer

Seal an exhaust vent

Dryer, bath or kitchen exhaust, or any HVAC exhaust. Fire, carbon monoxide and moisture risk.

Foam as exclusion

Rodents chew through it. Foam is a courtesy fix for gaps under ½ in only.

Touch gnawed wiring

No taping, splicing or "fixing" wires. A licensed electrician does that.

Trap or seal in wildlife

Squirrels, raccoons, opossums, bats. Never block an opening bats are using.

Walk tile or metal roofs

Tile cracks and metal is slick. Same rule as FC-06, no exceptions.

Licensed-trade repairs

Roofing, structural or electrical work. We are licensed for pest control, not construction.

Quote or promise

No prices, no "you're covered," no "they'll never come back."

Push past a stop

Unsafe access, heat or air quality ends the work for today. Finishing is never the reason to keep going.

01 · Rodent or wildlife?

Rodent Shield covers three animals. Everything else is wildlife.

House mice, roof rats and Norway rats. Tree squirrels are rodents too, but they are wildlife, not commensal rodents, and they are not Rodent Shield work.

Tree squirrels

Active by day. Round holes about 1½–2 in chewed through fascia or soffit; nut shells in the attic. Flying squirrels are active at night.

Raccoons

Torn-open soffits or roof vents, heavy thumping, crushed insulation, droppings piled in one latrine spot.

Bats

Dusk flights from a gap, staining at the opening, crumbly droppings piled below a roost. Protected. Never block them in.

Opossums

Under decks, sheds and slabs, sometimes in attics. Gray, cat-sized, bare tail. May hiss or play dead.

Armadillos, snakes, birds

Burrows at the foundation, a snake in the garage, birds nesting in a vent. Wildlife team.

What you do

Don't trap, handle or seal. Photograph from a distance, tell the customer the wildlife team handles it, and tell your branch manager the same day.

02 · Bats and maternity season

Bats are never harmed and never sealed in

Florida bats are protected. The Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission (FWC) allows bats out of a building only by exclusion with one-way devices, and never during maternity season, April 16 to August 14, when flightless young are inside.

Sealing a bat opening on a rodent job traps bats inside. They die in the structure or find their way into the living space, and it breaks state rules.
Guano: don't disturb, sweep or vacuum it; it can carry the fungus that causes histoplasmosis. Anyone bitten or scratched by a bat, or who touched one, needs medical or public-health advice right away.

04 · Health concerns and exposures

Give facts and the right number. Never a diagnosis.

"Can we get sick from this?"

Rodents can spread salmonella, leptospirosis and rat-bite fever. In Florida, hantavirus is linked to cotton rats and rice rats, not the house mice and rats we treat. Health questions go to their doctor or the county health department.

"My dog got into the bait"

Read them the product name, active ingredient and EPA Reg. No. from the label (482.2265). Call their vet or an animal poison control line now. Call your branch manager right away.

A child may have eaten bait

Trouble breathing, unresponsive or seizing: 911. Otherwise Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222, with the label in hand. Call your branch manager right away.

You were bitten or scratched

Wash with soap and water right away. Tell your supervisor immediately, get medical evaluation, and file the written report the same day.

Raccoon latrine or heavy guano

Don't disturb it. Raccoon droppings can carry roundworm eggs, and guano can carry histoplasmosis. Wildlife team, through your branch manager.

Heavy droppings, nesting, urine

Never sweep or vacuum dry. Wet it with disinfectant first, with PPE (FC-03). Large contamination is escalated, not cleaned up.

Stop-work and safety limits

01 · Your stop-work authority

If it isn't safe or can't be done right, the work stops

No customer, schedule or sale overrides it. Stopping is not a refusal: it protects you, the quality of the work and the warranty.

"We'll need to come back when this is addressed." Then document the reason and tell your supervisor the same day.
What stops the job
  • Unsafe access. Rotted decking, a ladder that can't be set or secured, a slippery or fragile roof.
  • Excessive heat. Florida attics pass 120°F. Dizziness, cramps or confusion mean get out now.
  • Air quality or ventilation. Strong unknown odor, no airflow, a space you can't safely get out of.
  • Electrical surprises. Live conductors where you didn't expect them, damaged wiring at the work area.
  • Customer interference that creates a safety problem, such as moving your ladder or letting pets loose.
  • Conditions outside authorized scope, or something you haven't been trained to handle.

03 · Electrical damage and attics

Two places where a rodent job turns dangerous

Wiring damaged by gnawing

  1. 1Don't touch it, and don't set traps or stations against it. Treat every wire as live.
  2. 2Smoke, burning smell or sparking: everyone out, and 911.
  3. 3Photograph it from a safe distance and note the location.
  4. 4Tell the customer today that a licensed electrician should inspect it.
  5. 5Branch manager, same day. Then technotes, so the office logs it.

Attics and crawlspaces

  • No attic access: we can't place interior traps (common in mobile homes). Escalate; don't cut an opening.
  • Blocked access: the customer clears it. Don't move heavy or valuable items yourself.
  • Walk on joists only. Sagging decking or weak joists end the inspection there.
  • Heat: plan attic work for the cooler morning, take water breaks, and come down at the first sign of heat illness.
  • PPE per FC-03: gloves, eye protection and a respirator where droppings or insulation are disturbed.

02 · Telling the customer

Frame it as protecting the work, not as a refusal

  1. 1Show what you found. The photo, the location, in plain words.
  2. 2Say why it matters. Sealing over it would fail, or it isn't safe today.
  3. 3Say what happens next and who will contact them: "Your branch manager will call you today."
  4. 4Don't fill the gap with a guess: no price, coverage decision or diagnosis.

"I want to make sure we do this right rather than fast. The rot in this section is beyond what I can properly seal. Sealing it now would just fail in six months. I'm going to flag this for our team, and we'll either come back to address it or coordinate with a roofer."CLEMENTS RODENT CERTIFICATION TRAINING

Say

  • "Here's what I found, and here's the photo."
  • "That's a job for a licensed electrician. Please have one look at it soon."
  • "Our wildlife team handles bats. Please don't block that opening."

Never say

  • "I'm not allowed to do that." (Explain why instead.)
  • "That'll be about..." or "That's covered."
  • "The last company did a terrible job."
03 · Disputes and warranty claims

You document. The branch manager decides.

An unhappy customer

  • Listen and repeat back what they're upset about. Stay calm; don't argue or defend.
  • Don't blame the customer, a co-worker or another company.
  • Don't promise refunds, credits, free visits or extra work. That's the branch manager's call.
  • Show your work: what you checked today, with photos.
  • Escalate the same day and tell them when to expect the call. Complaints belong to the branch manager.

A possible warranty claim

  • Plus only, on a 1-year or 3-year warranty. Basic carries no warranty.
  • Review is triggered by continued activity in treated areas, a missed entry point within the original scope, or new activity matching the original area.
  • Not covered: entry points the homeowner declined at the original service, and accounts lapsed past the 30-day grace period.
  • Your job: inspect, photograph against the original entry point record, document, escalate. Never say "covered" or "not covered."
04 · Documentation

If it isn't written down, it didn't happen

Every escalation record shows
  • What you found: location, size, condition
  • Photos: a wide shot, then a close-up with a scale reference in frame
  • Why you stopped or why it's out of scope
  • What you told the customer, and how they responded
  • Who you notified, and when
  • The plan: follow-up, referral, or return visit
