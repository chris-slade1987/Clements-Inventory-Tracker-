01 · Three questions

Every herbicide has three labels you can put on it

When: pre or post

Pre-emergent goes on before weeds sprout and stops seeds from establishing. Post-emergent goes on weeds you can see, while they're actively growing.

What: selective or not

Selective kills certain plants and spares others, like dollarweed out of St. Augustine. Non-selective injures nearly any plant it touches, turf included.

How: contact or systemic

Contact kills only the tissue it touches: fast burn, but roots can regrow. Systemic is absorbed and moves to the roots: slower to show, but a full kill.

A product can be all three at once. A typical broadleaf lawn spray is post-emergent, selective and systemic. The label tells you which, plus the weeds it controls and the grasses it's cleared for.
02 · Pre-emergents

A barrier for seeds, not a cure for weeds

  • Targets annuals from seed: crabgrass, goosegrass, spurge, chamberbitter, winter annuals.
  • Does nothing to weeds already up. Perennials like dollarweed and nutsedge need post-emergent work.
  • Must be activated by irrigation or rain, per the label.
  • Disturbing the soil breaks it. Aeration or digging after application opens gaps.

Timing in our part of Florida (UF/IFAS)

Spring, for crabgrass: down by Feb 1 in south Florida (Naples, Stuart) and Feb 15 in central Florida (Vero Beach, Orlando). Fall, for winter annuals: when nights drop to 55–60°F for several nights in a row.

03 · Contact vs systemic

Fast burn, or the whole plant

Many tank mixes pair both, so the customer sees quick browning and we still get the roots. Tell them up front: the browning comes fast, the full kill takes a couple of weeks.

01 · Turf first

Know the grass before you mix

LAWN-04 teaches how to identify each grass. Here's what each one means for herbicides. Select a turf.

Can't confirm the turf, or the lawn is a mix of grasses? Stop and ask before you spray. Treat a mixed lawn as the most sensitive grass in it.
02 · Heat, stress and timing

Hot, dry turf can't take what it tolerates in spring

85–90°F is the line. Many lawn herbicides are label-restricted above it, especially on St. Augustine. UF/IFAS: no atrazine on Floratam above 85°F.
Drought-stressed turf: wait. UF/IFAS advises against post-emergents when turf is under moisture stress. Stressed weeds don't absorb well either.
Summer on St. Augustine: spray early morning or late afternoon, not in the heat of the day.
Rainfast window: most post-emergents need 1–6 rain-free hours (check the label). Never spray ahead of heavy rain.
Mowing: most labels want no mowing 24–48 hours before and after, so there's leaf to absorb the spray.
New sod or sprigs: many herbicides aren't cleared for turf that hasn't rooted in. Read the label's establishment section.
03 · Off-target movement

Three ways herbicide ends up where you didn't put it

Drift

Fine droplets ride the wind onto roses, gardens, the neighbor's yard or water. Use coarse droplets and low pressure, keep the nozzle low, and follow FC-04's wind and inversion rules.

Volatility

Some herbicides turn to vapor after they land, especially in heat, and move as a gas long after a calm spray. Ester formulations and dicamba are the usual suspects. Heed the label's temperature limits.

Root uptake

Tree and shrub roots reach well under the lawn. Some turf herbicides are taken up by those roots. UF/IFAS warns weed-and-feed products can damage landscape plants this way.

Drift onto any person or property that wasn't meant to get it is a violation under FIFRA and Florida law, no matter how good your intentions were. If it happens, tell your supervisor immediately and write it up the same day.

02 · Ornamental beds

Beds are a different use site with different rules

Prevent first

A 2–3 inch layer of pine bark, wood chips or pine straw blocks light and stops many weeds. Pre-emergents in beds must be labeled for use around ornamentals (isoxaben-based products are common examples).

Grassy weeds in beds

Grass-selective herbicides such as fluazifop or sethoxydim spare most shrubs, but kill any turf they touch. Stay well back from the lawn edge.

Non-selective spot sprays

Glyphosate or similar products injure any green tissue they reach. Low pressure, coarse spray, no wind, and a shield between the nozzle and the plant.

Never use a turf herbicide in a bed unless the label lists ornamental beds as a use site. Keeping the rate low doesn't make it legal.
Pull by hand when there are only a few weeds. Florida betony and nutsedge need the whole tuber out or they come back.
03 · Setting expectations

Tell them what they'll see, and when

Today

Lawn notice posted. People and pets stay off until the spray has dried, or longer if the label says so.

Days 2 to 5

Weeds start to yellow, curl or brown. Contact products show it sooner.

Days 7 to 14

Full kill from systemic products. Tough perennials like nutsedge or torpedograss may need repeat visits.

Weeks after

Healthy turf slowly fills the gaps. New seed keeps germinating, which is why the program has regular visits.

Name the weed. "That's doveweed, and it loves hot, wet weather" builds more trust than "weeds." Explain your method in one sentence.
Never call a herbicide safe or harmless, and never promise a result. Give the label's re-entry instruction, and send pricing or upgrade questions to the branch manager.
