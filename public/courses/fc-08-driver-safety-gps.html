04 · Seat belts and fatigue

Buckled, awake and sober, or don't drive

Seat belts

Florida requires the driver, front-seat passengers and every passenger under 18 to buckle up (s. 316.614). Clements goes further: everyone riding in a company vehicle or on company business wears one. A missing or broken belt gets reported to your supervisor immediately.

Fatigue

Warning signs: yawning, heavy eyelids, drifting, hitting rumble strips, missing a turn, not remembering the last few miles. Florida heat and a big lunch make it worse. Caffeine and the radio don't fix it. Pull over somewhere safe and call your supervisor if you're too tired to drive.

Impairment

No alcohol or drugs in, or while driving, a company vehicle. Tell management before you drive about any medication that may affect your judgment or driving, including drowsiness warnings on over-the-counter products.

Only Clements employees ride in a company vehicle. No friends, family or customers, ever.
Part two

Drive defensively

You can't control other drivers, the weather or a kid chasing a ball into the street. You can control your speed, your space and your attention, and that's what keeps a mistake by someone else from becoming your crash.

01 · Space, speed and scanning

Expect the unexpected, and leave room for it

Scan

Look far down the road, not at the bumper ahead. Check mirrors every few seconds. At intersections, look left, right and left again, even on a green light. Watch for brake lights two or three cars up.

Space

Keep at least 3–4 seconds behind the vehicle ahead, more in rain or with a heavy load. Leave an escape route to the side. If someone tailgates you, ease off and let them pass.

Speed

Posted limits are the maximum for ideal conditions. Slow down in neighborhoods, school zones and around stopped vehicles. Running late is never a reason to speed. Call the office from a parked truck instead.

Lane changes: signal, check mirrors, then a quick head check for the blind spot. A work truck has bigger blind spots than a car.
Move Over: for any vehicle stopped on the roadside with hazard lights, flares or emergency signs, move over a lane. If you can't, slow to 20 mph below the limit (s. 316.126).
02 · Practice · the following-distance timer

03 · Florida weather

Afternoon storms are part of the route. Plan for them.

Rain

Slow down and open your following distance to 5 seconds or more. Headlights on in any rain, smoke or fog (s. 316.217). Hazards while moving only in extremely low visibility on roads posted 55 mph or more (s. 316.2397). Can't see? Pull well off the road and wait.

Hydroplaning

Tires can ride up on a film of water at speeds as low as about 35 mph, and worn tires make it worse. No cruise control on wet roads. If the steering goes light: ease off the gas, keep the wheel straight, don't brake hard, and let the tires find the road.

Flooded roads

Just 12 inches of moving water can carry away most cars, and 2 feet can carry away SUVs and trucks (National Weather Service). You can't see washouts or how deep it is. Turn around, don't drown. Call the office and reroute.

Sun glare at sunrise and late afternoon can blind you at an intersection. Keep the windshield clean inside and out, use the visor and sunglasses, and slow down when you can't see the signal.

01 · Backing: the number-one fleet incident

Avoid it. If you can't, Get Out And Look.

Backing is the most common fleet incident. Backover crashes kill about 210 people and injure 15,000 a year in the U.S., most often young children and older adults (NHTSA).

  • Plan not to back. Choose spots you can pull through or out of forward.
  • Back in on arrival when you must back at all (handbook). You know the space is clear right then, and you leave forward into traffic.
  • GOAL every time: walk around and check behind, under and overhead: kids, pets, toys, posts, low branches, wires.
  • Use a spotter when one is available. Stay where you can see them in your mirror, and stop if you lose sight of them.
  • Camera and sensors help but don't replace looking. Back slowly, as short a distance as possible.

03 · Parking at the customer's home

Park like a guest, and like someone who plans to leave safely

Do

  • Park legally, in the direction of traffic, where you can pull out forward
  • Set the parking brake. Turn wheels to the curb on a slope
  • Lock the truck every time you step away (handbook: never left unlocked)
  • Watch for children, pets and bikes when you arrive and leave
  • Drive neighborhood streets slowly. Kids and pets don't wait for a gap

Don't

  • Block driveways, mailboxes, fire hydrants, sidewalks or crosswalks
  • Park on lawns or landscaping. Our trucks are heavy and ruts cost money
  • Leave equipment, hoses or product unattended outside the truck
  • Park where you'll have to back into a busy road or a blind curve
  • Leave the engine running with the truck unlocked

Some communities have their own rules on commercial vehicles and street parking. Follow posted signs and any instructions on the customer's account.

04 · Securing the load

In a sudden stop, everything loose keeps going

At highway speed, a 20-pound toolbox that comes loose can hit with the force of a 1,000-pound object (New Mexico DOT). Florida law requires every load to be secured so it can't fall, shift, leak or blow off (s. 316.520).

A spilled load is also a pesticide spill. Contain it, protect the public and follow spill procedures (FC‑05).

Tanks and sprayers

Mounted tanks bolted and checked. Backpack and hand sprayers strapped upright, lids tight.

Ladders

Tied to the rack at both ends. Check the straps before every trip, not just in the morning.

Product

In the locked storage box, upright and closed. Never in the cab with you (FC‑05, FC‑07).

The cab

Phone and tablet mounted or stowed. Nothing on the dash or seat. No bottles near the pedals.

01 · What the GPS records, and why

Your truck keeps a record of every drive

What it records

  • Location and the route you drove
  • Speed
  • Harsh braking and rapid acceleration
  • Route efficiency: miles, stops and time between them
  • Typically also ignition on/off, idling and time at each address
Keep it on. Never unplug, cover, block or tamper with the GPS unit. If it seems to be malfunctioning, tell your supervisor.

Safety

Speeding and harsh events get coached before they become crashes.

Routing

Real drive times help build tighter routes with less windshield time (FC‑09).

Accountability

Shows when we arrived and left, settles disputes and claims, and helps recover a stolen truck.

It's part of your performance. GPS data factors into your evaluations. Safe driving counts in your favor; risky driving can lead to coaching or discipline.

Your branch manager can show you the system and the reports used at your branch.

03 · Accidents and breakdowns

Stop. Make it safe. Call. Document.

If you have an accident

  1. 1Stop at once. Anyone hurt? Call 911.
  2. 2No injuries and drivable? Move out of the travel lanes (s. 316.061).
  3. 3Call the police for every accident, and stay until they release you.
  4. 4Call your supervisor and HR right away.

Then document

  1. 5Photograph the damage and the scene.
  2. 6Collect names, license, insurance and witness details. Complete the vehicle accident form.
  3. 7Don't discuss fault with anyone but the police.
  4. 8Product spilled? Contain it and follow spill procedures.

If the truck breaks down

  • Signal and get as far off the road as you can
  • Hazard lights on. Florida's Move Over law now covers you too, but don't count on every driver
  • Stay clear of traffic. Exit away from the travel lane
  • Call your supervisor, then the office at 772-562-6450

Full accident and injury reporting is covered in CW-06 · Company Policies and CW-09 · Safety & Accident Reporting.
