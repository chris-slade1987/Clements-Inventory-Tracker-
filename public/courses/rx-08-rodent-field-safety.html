Rodent Field Safety

Rodent jobs put you in hot attics, on rooflines and around droppings, urine and nests. This is how you do the work and go home healthy, every time.

45–60 min5 checkpoints20-question check · 80% to passDraft · needs Clements review
Course objectives

By the end of this course you will be able to:

Name the diseases rodents carry, how each one reaches you, and which rodents carry hantavirus in Florida
Clean up droppings, urine and nests the CDC way: ventilate, wet, wait, wipe, bag. Never sweep or vacuum
Handle dead rodents and traps safely, and decontaminate before you get back in the truck
Pick the right gloves, eyewear, coveralls and respirator (N95 or P100) for each rodent task
Work an attic safely: heat, joists, wiring, nails, insulation and the roofline
Respond to a bite, scratch, needle-stick or illness, and report it the same day
Why rodent safety matters

The hottest, highest and dirtiest work we do.

A Rodent Shield job can stack a biohazard, a 130°F attic and an extension ladder into one morning. None of it is theoretical. Skipping a step doesn't save twenty minutes. It can end a career.

Droppings are a biohazard

Rodent urine, droppings and nests carry bacteria and viruses that cause serious illness. Some, untreated, kill.

OSHA expects it

Clements must assess hazards, provide PPE and train you (29 CFR 1910.132). Respirators have their own standard (1910.134).

The customer's family lives there

Nest debris carried through a hallway, or dust kicked out of an attic, lands on their floors. Doing it right protects them too.

On every rodent job

Four kinds of hazard, often in the same hour

Biological

Droppings, urine, nests, dead rodents, fleas, bites and scratches. Part one and two.

Heat

Florida attics regularly pass 130°F in summer, and PPE traps body heat. Part three.

Falls

Through a ceiling between joists, off a ladder at the eave, off a roof. Part three and FC-06.

Physical

Gnawed wiring, roofing nails, cut metal, insulation fibers and discarded needles. Part three and four.

Stop-work authority is yours. If heat, access, air, wiring or anything else makes the job unsafe, you stop, document why, tell the customer professionally and call your supervisor. RX-09 covers escalation.

What rodents carry

01 · Exposure routes

Four ways a rodent pathogen gets into you

Breathing it in. Dried droppings, urine and nesting material break into dust when disturbed. Sweeping, vacuuming and crawling through insulation put it in the air.
Skin breaks and mucous membranes. Urine-contaminated water or soil entering a cut, or splashed into eyes, nose or mouth.
Hand to mouth. Touching droppings, a trap or a dead rodent, then eating, drinking, smoking or touching your face.
Bites and scratches. Live rodents in traps, under debris or cornered in a garage.

03 · Hantavirus in Florida

Get it right: which rodents carry it here

Linked to hantavirus in Florida
  • Hispid cotton rat. Native, lives in tall grass, fields and yard edges statewide. Host of Black Creek Canal virus, linked to Florida's first documented hantavirus pulmonary syndrome case (Dade County, 1993).
  • Marsh rice rat. Native, lives in wetlands and marsh edges across the Southeast. Host of Bayou virus.
  • Both can wander into garages, sheds and crawl spaces next to fields and water.
Our three household rodents
  • House mouse, roof rat, Norway rat are not known carriers of the hantaviruses that cause pulmonary syndrome in the US.
  • Norway rats can carry Seoul virus, a different hantavirus that affects the kidneys. US cases are rare.
  • They do carry leptospirosis, salmonella, rat-bite fever and LCMV, plus allergens in their dust.
Same protocol on every job. You can't be certain which rodent left the droppings, and the household rodents carry plenty of other disease. If a customer asks about hantavirus, give the accurate picture calmly and refer health questions to their doctor or the health department.

Clean it up and carry it out

Why it matters: most exposure happens when droppings are disturbed, a dead rodent is handled or dirty gear goes back in the truck. The right order turns a hazard into a routine task.

01 · Before you start

Know your scope, mix it right, air it out

Scope

We clean up small amounts at our work area: droppings by a trap, at an entry point, under a sink. Roughly 2 by 2 feet or less. Bigger contamination, or soaked insulation, is not a Clements cleanup: photograph it and tell your supervisor (RX-09).

Disinfectant

1 part bleach to 9 parts water (about 1½ cups per gallon) or an EPA-registered disinfectant used per its label. Mix bleach fresh. Disinfectants are registered pesticides: the label is the law.

Ventilate

CDC: in a closed room, shed or garage, open doors and windows for 30 minutes and step out before you start. In an attic, move slowly and don't stir up material while it airs.

Never sweep, vacuum, blow or pressure-wash dry droppings or nests. That puts the dust in the air you breathe. Wet it first, every time.
Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners. It releases toxic gas. Mix bleach fresh, with water only, and test on finished surfaces.
02 · Practice · CDC cleanup steps

Put the cleanup in the right order

Select the steps in the order you'd do them. You found a small pile of mouse droppings and some urine staining beside a trap in a garage.

The wait matters. Five minutes (or the label's contact time) gives the disinfectant time to work before anything is wiped.
03 · Dead rodents and traps

Gloves on, spray, wait, double-bag

  1. 1Gloves on before you touch a carcass, a trap or a station. Never bare-handed, never close to your face.
  2. 2Spray the rodent, nest or trap and the area around it until wet. Wait 5 minutes.
  3. 3Bag it, knot the bag, then place it in a second bag and knot that too.
  4. 4Reusing a snap trap? Soak it in disinfectant for 5 minutes, release the rodent over the bag, rinse and let it dry.
  5. 5Gloves off inside out, into the bag, then wash your hands with soap and water.
Fleas leave a dead host. Insect repellent on clothing and shoes before you handle dead rodents and nests helps keep them off you.
Baited account? Follow the rodenticide label on carcass search and disposal. Collecting dead rodents on follow-ups also protects pets and wildlife from secondary poisoning.
A dead bird, pet or other non-target animal near a station: don't handle it bare-handed, photograph it, and tell your supervisor the same day.
Live bats, raccoons, snakes: never handle them. Back away and escalate (RX-09).
04 · Decontamination

Nothing contaminated rides in the cab

At the work area

Coveralls and outer gloves come off before you leave the attic or crawl space opening, rolled inside out.

Bag and seal

Disposable PPE, nest material and towels go into sealed bags before you walk through the living space. Never loose debris down a hallway.

Boots and tools

Wipe boot soles and hand tools with disinfectant. Respirator facepiece cleaned and stored in a sealed bag (FC-03).

Wash up

Hands and forearms with soap and water before you eat, drink, smoke or use your phone. Heavy exposure: shower and change.

Work clothes wash separately from family laundry, hot water and detergent. A torn coverall that let dust through: bag that clothing and wash it separately.
Sealed waste bags never go in the cab. Dispose of them the way your supervisor directs for your branch.

PPE, the attic and the roofline

Why it matters: the attic is where droppings pile up, heat builds and one wrong step goes through a ceiling. The roofline is where roof rats get in, and where falls happen.

01 · Respirators

Particulate protection, sealed to your face

N95 · the minimum

NIOSH-approved filtering facepiece. Required before entering an attic or crawl space on an active or recent rodent account, and for trap checks in enclosed spaces with built-up droppings.

Half-face P100 · cleanup

Elastomeric half mask with P100 (magenta) filters. For nest removal, cleanup and heavy contamination. This is what CDC and NIOSH point to for heavy rodent work.

Not a respirator

Paper dust masks, surgical masks and bandanas. They don't seal and aren't rated. And particulate filters don't stop pesticide vapors: if you treat too, follow the label (FC-03).

1 · Medical clearance before your first use
2 · Fit test before first use and every year, on the exact model and size
3 · Clean-shaven where it seals. Stubble breaks the seal
4 · Seal check every time it goes on: positive and negative

03 · Attic and crawl space entry

Before, during, and when to get out

Before

  • Someone knows you're going in, with a check-in time
  • Check the attic ladder (FC-06) and the hatch area
  • PPE on and seal-checked; water first
  • Headlamp or flashlight. Never an open flame
  • Smell gas or sewer, see heavy mold or deteriorated pipe wrap? Don't enter. Call your supervisor

During

  • Joists or walk boards only. Insulation and drywall won't hold you
  • Move slowly, head low: truss webs and roofing nails
  • Don't step on ducts, lights or the air handler
  • Hands off wiring. Gnawed wire is a fire and shock hazard
  • Keep your path back to the hatch clear

Get out when

  • Dizzy, nauseous, headache, cramping or confused
  • Your respirator leaks, or you smell something new
  • Wiring is exposed or sparking
  • Structure feels soft or sags
  • Wasps, a bat colony or a cornered animal
Confined space awareness. Attics and crawl spaces can meet OSHA's confined space definition (1910.146): hard to get out of, not built for occupancy. Crawl spaces go in feet-first, never head-first. A second person at the opening is the safest setup, especially in heat or heavy contamination.
03 · Attic heat

An attic can be 40°F hotter than the yard

  • Exclusion and attic work early in the day in summer
  • Water before entry, then about every 15 to 20 minutes
  • Short attic stints with cool-down breaks
  • New to the work or back from time off? Ease in: bodies take 1 to 2 weeks to acclimate

Heat cramps

Painful cramps in legs or belly. Get out, cool off, drink water. Don't go back in until they're gone.

Heat exhaustion

Heavy sweating, cool clammy skin, headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness. Stop. Shade or AC, loosen PPE, sip water, tell your supervisor. Not better within an hour, or worse? Get medical care.

Heat stroke

Confusion, slurred speech, fainting, seizures, very high temperature, hot skin. Call 911. Cool them right away with water, ice and shade and stay with them.

Never pull your respirator off to cool down in a dusty attic. Get out of the attic first, then take your PPE off safely.
The rule today: there is no final federal OSHA heat standard yet. OSHA enforces heat hazards under the General Duty Clause, targets heat in its inspections, and expects water, rest, shade and acclimatization.
04 · Ladders and rooflines

Roof rats get in up high. Get up there the FC-06 way.

The ladder

  • Inspect it every shift; tag out any defect
  • Fiberglass, 10 feet from overhead lines
  • 4-to-1 angle, 3 feet above the edge, secured
  • Three points of contact; belt buckle inside the rails

Materials up high

  • Snips, screws and gun in a belt or pouch
  • Hardware cloth, sheet metal and cement go up on a hand line, not in your hand
  • Cut-resistant gloves and safety glasses for cut metal and mesh

The roof

  • Only with roof access cleared by your supervisor
  • Not wet, dewy, tile, metal or steep
  • Fall protection near an edge at 4 feet or more (OSHA 1910.28)
  • Soft decking or loose tile: stop
Soffits and gable vents hide wasps. Look before your face is at the gap. Never climb a palm or tree to reach the roof: that's a referral, not a shortcut.
Thunder? Off the ladder and into the truck. Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder (FC-06).

When something gets through

Why it matters: rodent-borne illness responds best to early treatment, and a doctor can only treat what they know about. What you do in the first hour, and who you tell, makes the difference.

01 · Bites, scratches, needle-sticks

Wash it. Tell your supervisor. Get it checked.

  1. 1Wash right away with soap and running water for several minutes. Apply antiseptic and cover it.
  2. 2Tell your supervisor immediately, however small it looks.
  3. 3Medical evaluation the same day. Rat-bite fever is treatable early, and tetanus status matters. A needle-stick needs care right away.
  4. 4Write it down: time, place, the animal or object, how it happened.
  5. 5Written report the same day, even if you feel fine (CW-09).
Any bat contact, or a bite from a raccoon or other wildlife: possible rabies exposure. Wash it and get medical care now. Rats and mice almost never carry rabies.

Sick in the weeks after a rodent job?

Fever, chills, muscle aches, rash, vomiting or shortness of breath. See a doctor and say you work around rodents. Then tell your supervisor. Hantavirus can take up to 8 weeks to show, leptospirosis up to 4.

Same rule as every injury, exposure or spill at Clements: supervisor immediately, written report the same day.
02 · Sharps and cuts

Sharp things hide in insulation, crawl spaces and debris

Discarded needles

Don't pick it up or move it. Photograph it, keep others away, tell the customer and your supervisor, and work around it only if you safely can. Needles can carry bloodborne disease.

Cut metal and mesh

Fresh-cut hardware cloth and sheet metal edges slice through nitrile. Cut-resistant work gloves for cutting and installing, nitrile underneath when there's contamination.

Look before you reach

Never reach blind into insulation, a wall void or debris. Look first with your light, or use a tool. Broken glass, nails, wasps and live animals all hide there.

Cut or punctured on the job? Same steps as a bite: wash, cover, tell your supervisor immediately, get it checked, and file the written report the same day.
