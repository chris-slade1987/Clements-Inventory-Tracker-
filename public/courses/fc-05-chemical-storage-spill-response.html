Why this course matters

Most chemical accidents start before the job does.

An unlocked toolbox, a loose jug on a hard stop, a concentrate poured into a drink bottle. Storage is where accidents are prevented. Spill response is where a bad moment stays small.

FDACS can inspect your truck

Florida inspectors check service vehicles for secured concentrates and properly identified containers (Rule 5E-14.106). A violation can lead to FDACS action against the company license and the people involved.

Storm drains go straight to water

Florida's storm drains empty into canals, lagoons and the Indian River with no treatment. A spill that reaches one can kill fish and bring significant fines.

Customers trust us around their families

Children, pets and neighbors walk past our trucks every day. A customer who sees an open toolbox or a puddle under the tailgate stops trusting what we put in their home.

02 · Containers and labels

Original container first. Labeled, always.

  1. 1Keep product in the original, labeled container whenever you can. The label is the law, and it travels with the product.
  2. 2A service container (anything other than the original jug or your application equipment) needs a permanent, durable label naming the active ingredient. Florida Rule 5E-14.106(4).
  3. 3Never put pesticide in a drink bottle, food container or cup. Children and coworkers have been poisoned this way.
  4. 4Mark every water container "H2O" so no one mistakes product for water, or water for product.
Example · service container label

CAUTION

Product: [Brand name of the concentrate]

Active ingredient: common or chemical name (required by Florida rule), plus percentage

EPA Reg. No.: from the original label

Mix rate and date mixed if it holds a dilution

Keep out of reach of children. Clements Pest Control. Carry the full product label and SDS on the truck.

03 · Heat and shifting loads

Heat and a hard stop are the two biggest threats on the road

Keep it out of the heat

A closed truck in a Florida summer gets far hotter than the air outside. Heat breaks product down, builds pressure in jugs and can burst aerosols. Follow the label's storage section and keep product shaded.

Secure it against shifting

Caps tight, containers upright and leak-tight, held in racks, bins or straps so nothing can slide, tip or be punctured in a hard stop or a crash.

Check it every day

On your walk-around, look for leaks, damaged containers, a loose strap or a lock that won't catch. A slow drip in the toolbox becomes a spill on the road.

The law vs. our standard: Florida requires concentrates to be locked whenever the truck is unattended. Clements goes further: the toolbox stays locked and the truck is never left unlocked, for any reason.
Only carry what the route needs. Order weekly on the chemical request form, and record anything you take from the warehouse or move between trucks. Less product on board means less to spill.

01 · The storage room standard

Secure, ventilated, separated, signed and counted

Secure

Locked, with access limited to trained staff. Away from break rooms, food, drinking water and office supplies. Exits, extinguishers and the eyewash kept clear.

Ventilated and dry

Airflow so vapors don't build up; cool and dry so containers don't swell, rust or cake. Liquids on containment trays below eye level. Bags on pallets.

Separated

Flammables (aerosols, solvent-based products) locked in the safety cabinet. Herbicides kept apart from insecticides and fertilizer so one can't contaminate another. Damaged containers isolated, not mixed back in.

Signed

Posted at the door: pesticide storage, authorized personnel only, no smoking, eating or drinking. The sign tells firefighters what's inside before they open the door.

Counted

First in, first out, so nothing expires on the shelf. Every withdrawal goes on the chemical sheet. An accurate inventory is what responders need in a fire.

02 · The 3 Cs · protect yourself first

Control it. Contain it. Clean it up.

01 · Control

Stop the flow

Keep people and pets away. Put on PPE from the label. Then stop the source if it's safe: set the jug upright, tighten the cap, close the valve, shut off the pump, or put a leaking container in a bucket or bag.

02 · Contain

Stop the spread

Ring the spill with absorbent socks or a dike of absorbent. Block any storm drain, ditch or water first. Never hose it down: water spreads pesticide and carries it to the drain.

03 · Clean up

Absorb, bag, decontaminate

Cover with absorbent, work it from the outside in, sweep or shovel into heavy plastic bags and seal them. Wash the surface with detergent and a little water, and absorb that too.

04 · Small spill or large spill?

Size decides who handles it. Every spill gets reported.

Small spill · you handle it

  • You can stop it, contain it and clean it up with your truck kit
  • Nothing has reached a drain, ditch, canal or pond
  • No one is hurt and there is no fire or vapor problem
  • Then: call your supervisor and file the report the same day

Large spill · stop and call

  • More than 5 gallons of any product
  • Any amount in a storm drain, ditch or water
  • On a public road, or more than your kit can hold
  • Anyone hurt, a fire, strong fumes, or you're not sure
  • Then: keep people back, contain only if safe, call your supervisor now, and 911 if anyone is in danger
Any doubt at all, treat it as large and call. Nobody at Clements will fault you for calling about a spill that turned out to be small.

People first, then your supervisor, then the paperwork

03 · Disposing of cleanup material

Used absorbent is pesticide waste. Treat it that way.

Bag and seal

Absorbent, contaminated soil, pads and disposable PPE into heavy plastic bags. Double-bag, seal and close the kit bucket.

Label it

Product name, "spill cleanup," date and your name, so no one mistakes it for trash.

Bring it back

Secured in the bed, away from the cab. Hand it to your supervisor, who disposes of it per the label, SDS Section 13 and local rules.

Clean and restock

Wash tools and boots with detergent. Restock the kit before your next route.

Never put cleanup waste in a customer's trash can, a dumpster, a storm drain or down a sink, and never leave it on site.
Clothing soaked with concentrate can't be cleaned reliably. Bag it, tell your supervisor and discard it, as covered in FC-03.
