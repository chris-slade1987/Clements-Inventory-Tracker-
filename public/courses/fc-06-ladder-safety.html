Ladder Safety

Pick the right ladder, inspect it, set it at the right angle, climb it the right way, and know when the answer is not to go up at all.

Course objectives

By the end of this course you will be able to:

Choose the right ladder type, length and duty rating for the job
Inspect a ladder before use and tag out one that is damaged
Set up at the 4-to-1 angle with 3 feet above the landing, on firm, level ground
Climb and work with three points of contact, inside the rails, below the highest standing level
Spot electrical, weather, attic and roof hazards, and know when not to go up
Secure, transport and store ladders, and respond to a fall correctly
Why ladder safety matters

The ladder is the most dangerous tool on your truck.

It doesn't look like it. There is no label, no signal word, no PPE statement. But falls are one of the leading causes of workplace death in the US, and you don't need to be high up: a fall from a few rungs onto concrete can end a career.

About 1 in 5 work fall injuries involve a ladder

CDC/NIOSH data. Most come from the same few mistakes: the wrong ladder, a bad angle, overreaching, and missing the last step.

OSHA sets the rules

29 CFR 1910.23 covers every ladder we use: inspection every shift, load limits, angle, extension above the landing, and facing the ladder.

Customers are watching

A ladder through a screen, a cracked roof tile or a gouged gutter costs us trust and money. A careful setup tells the customer we know what we're doing.

On the job

Where a Clements technician ends up on a ladder

Attic access

Rodent inspections, trapping, exclusion checks, attic dusting. Often the customer's pull-down ladder in a garage or closet.

Eaves and soffits

Wasp and bee nests, roof rat entry points, gaps at the roofline, gutters and fascia.

Commercial ceilings

Drop ceilings, fly lights, overhead pipes and storage racks in restaurants and warehouses.

Garages and lanais

High shelves, garage door tracks, screen enclosure frames and pool cages.

The Clements rule comes first: if you don't feel safe, don't use the ladder. Stop, and call your supervisor. Nobody will ever be faulted for asking.

01 · Ladder types

Match the ladder to the job, not to what's handy

Stepladder

Self-supporting, for work in open floor space. Fully open, spreaders locked, on level ground. Never lean a closed stepladder against a wall like a straight ladder.

Extension ladder

Leans on a support for reaching eaves, soffits and rooflines. Needs the 4-to-1 angle and must be secured. Talk to your supervisor before using one.

Multi-position

Converts between stepladder, extension and other modes. Each mode has its own rules; every lock must click in before you climb.

Attic ladder

Built into the customer's ceiling. Not ours, not inspected by us, often worn. Check it before you trust it (Part three).

Fiberglass for field work. Aluminum conducts electricity. Near any power line, service drop or electrical panel, use only a clean, dry fiberglass ladder.
Never substitute. Chairs, buckets, boxes, the truck bumper, a customer's patio furniture: none of them are ladders. Never set a ladder on top of one, either.
02 · Duty ratings

The rating is everything you put on the ladder

OSHA: never load a ladder beyond its maximum intended load. That load is you, your clothes and boots, your tool belt, and whatever you carry. Find the rating on the label on the side rail.

04 · Practice · pre-use inspection

These two ladders have eight defects. Find them.

Select any part that looks wrong. Some parts are fine. OSHA requires an inspection before each shift's first use, and again after anything that could damage the ladder.

Found 0 of 8

Start with the rails, feet and steps.

Inspection · every shift

Look at it, work it, then trust it

Look

  • Rails: no cracks, splits, bends, dents or burn marks
  • Steps and rungs tight, straight, free of oil, grease, mud and pesticide residue
  • Both safety feet present, pads not worn smooth
  • Labels readable: duty rating and highest standing level

Work it

  • Spreaders open fully and lock
  • Rung locks and pulley move freely and seat fully
  • Rope not frayed, cut or stiff
  • No loose rivets or bolts, no wobble
  • Fiberglass not chalky or showing loose fibers ("bloom")

Found a defect?

  • Stop. Don't use it, even for one quick job
  • Tag it "Dangerous: Do Not Use" right away (OSHA)
  • Tell your supervisor and get a replacement
  • Never repair it yourself with tape, wire or paint
Don't paint a ladder. Paint hides cracks. Markings for identification only, on the side rail, so the inspection label and the rail stay visible.
Part two

Set it up right

A good ladder set up badly is a bad ladder. The angle, the footing and what's overhead decide whether it stays put once you're on it.

01 · Survey before you set

Thirty seconds of looking saves the fall

Footing

Firm, level and dry. Florida sand, wet sod and loose pavers shift under load. Use a leveler or a wide base board where allowed; never boxes, blocks or bricks.

Doors and traffic

In a doorway, passageway or driveway, lock or block the door, or barricade and post a sign. Tell the customer you're working there.

Overhead

Power lines, service drops, light fixtures, ceiling fans, gutters that won't bear weight, and active nests. Look up before the ladder goes up.

People and pets

Keep kids, pets and the customer out from under the ladder. A dog bumping the base can knock it out.

Sprinklers and wet surfaces

Check for irrigation heads that may come on, and wet pool decks. OSHA: on slippery surfaces, the ladder must be secured and stabilized.

Wind and weather

Gusts push an extension ladder sideways. If the wind is moving the ladder or you, don't climb (Part three).

02 · Electrical hazards

Ten feet from any overhead line. Always.

OSHA requires unqualified workers to keep themselves and anything conductive at least 10 feet from overhead lines (more for lines over 50 kV). That includes the ladder while you carry it, raise it and lower it.

  • Assume every line is live, including the service drop to the house
  • Carry and raise the ladder away from lines; look up the whole time
  • Fiberglass only near electricity, and only clean and dry: dirt and moisture conduct
  • Can't keep 10 feet? Don't set up. Call your supervisor; the utility handles it
If someone contacts a line: don't touch them or the ladder. Call 911, keep everyone back, then call your supervisor.
03 · The 4-to-1 rule

1 foot out for every 4 feet up

Measure the height to where the ladder touches the support (the gutter or wall), not the ladder's length. Divide by 4: that's how far the feet sit from the wall. It puts the ladder at about 75 degrees.

04 · Practice · extension ladder setup

Put the setup in the right order

Select the steps in the order you'd do them. One wrong step and the ladder is up in the wrong place, at the wrong angle, or unsecured.

Never extend, shift or move a ladder while anyone is on it, and never tie two ladders together for more length.
05 · Stepladder setup

Fully open, locked, level, square to the work

  1. 1Open it all the way and lock both spreaders. A half-open stepladder folds under you.
  2. 2All four feet flat on firm, level ground. Rock it: no wobble.
  3. 3Face the work with the steps toward it, close enough that you don't reach.
  4. 4Climb the front only, unless it is a two-sided ladder built for it. Never straddle the top.
  5. 5Nothing left on steps or the top cap. The pail shelf holds a pail, not a person.

Length rule of thumb

Standing where you're allowed, you can reach about 3 feet above the ladder's height. Ceiling at 9 feet: a 6-foot stepladder. At 11 feet: an 8-foot. If you need the top steps, get a taller ladder.

Never stand on the top cap or the top step. OSHA prohibits both, and the Clements rule goes further: stay off the top two steps. Part three shows exactly where that leaves you.
Part three

Climb it and work from it

Most falls happen while climbing or reaching, not from a ladder that fails. How you move on the ladder matters as much as how it's set.

01 · Climbing

Three points on the ladder. Belt buckle between the rails.

  • Three points of contact: two hands and a foot, or two feet and a hand, at all times. Move one limb at a time.
  • Face the ladder going up and coming down. OSHA requires it.
  • Hands free. Tools in a belt or pouch; raise the sprayer or duster with a hand line, or have it handed up. Nothing in your hands that could pull you off balance.
  • Belt-buckle rule: keep your belt buckle (your center) inside the rails. If you have to lean past a rail, climb down and move the ladder.
  • Clean, slip-resistant boots. Wipe off wet grass, mud and product before you step on.
  • One person on a ladder at a time, unless it's built for two.

The label marks the manufacturer's highest standing level. On a stepladder the Clements rule (off the top two steps) can be stricter: follow whichever is lower. On an extension ladder, nothing above the 4th rung from the top.
03 · Treating overhead

A wasp nest on a ladder is two hazards at once

Treat from the ground first

An extension wand, pole duster or aerosol with a long straw often reaches eaves and soffits without a ladder at all. The safest climb is the one you don't make.

If you must climb, plan the exit

Set the ladder to the side of the nest, not under it, so a startled colony doesn't send you backward. Know your way down before you treat.

PPE for overhead work

Product falls toward your face. Wear eye protection on every overhead application, plus whatever the label requires (FC-03).

One hand for the ladder

Working with one hand means the other holds the ladder. Keep the belt buckle inside the rails. Don't lean back to spray.

Allergic to stings, or the colony is large and active? Don't climb to it. Call your supervisor for a plan.
04 · Attic ladders and roofs

Know when not to go up

The customer's attic ladder

  • Open it fully. Legs flat on the floor, the sections in a straight line, hinges locked
  • Check for loose bolts, cracked steps, a hatch spring that is broken, legs cut too short or too long
  • Check the rating label. Older units are often 250 lb or less
  • Climb facing it. Hand gear up on a second trip or set it in the hatch first
  • Loose, cracked or wobbling? Use our own stepladder and email technotes@clementspestcontrol.com

Don't go up when

  • You haven't cleared roof access with your supervisor
  • The roof is wet, dewy, steep, tile or metal: tile cracks and metal is slick
  • The ladder can't be secured or can't extend 3 feet above the edge
  • Wind is gusting, or there's thunder or lightning
  • You're near the edge without fall protection: OSHA requires it at 4 feet or more
  • You don't feel safe. That alone is enough
Attic heat hits balance first. Florida attics can pass 120°F. Climb down slowly after attic work, and stop if you feel dizzy.
05 · Florida weather

The forecast is part of the setup

Lightning

Hear thunder? Get off the ladder and into the truck. Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder before you go back up. Afternoon storms come fast.

Wind

Gusts catch an extension ladder like a sail, during raising and while you're on it. If it's moving the ladder or you, wait or reschedule.

Rain and dew

Wet rungs, wet boots, wet sod. Morning dew on a roof or a ladder is as slick as rain. Dry the rungs and your soles before climbing.

Heat

Aluminum gets hot enough to burn in the sun. Fatigue and dehydration cost you balance. Drink water and take breaks.

Rescheduling a ladder job for weather is the right call, not a missed stop. Tell your supervisor and the office so the customer hears from us.
Part four

Transport, storage, and when it goes wrong

A ladder that flies off a truck on I-95 is as dangerous as one that slips off a gutter. And when someone does fall, the first minutes matter.

01 · On the truck and in the warehouse

Tie it down at both ends, every time you drive

On the rack

  • Locked into the rack and strapped at front and rear
  • Walk around and check it before every drive, not just in the morning
  • Florida law: a load must not shift, fall or blow off
  • Rack latches and straps are part of your truck inspection

Rear overhang

  • Sticking out 4 feet or more past the back of the truck needs a red flag at least 18 inches square at the end
  • At night: red lamps and reflectors instead (FS 316.228)
  • Better: a rack that keeps it inside the truck's length

Storage

  • Warehouse: on the wall, secured with a ratchet strap so it can't fall
  • Keep fiberglass out of constant sun when you can; UV breaks it down
  • Away from heat, chemicals and traffic
  • Tagged ladders stay apart until repaired or discarded
Carrying it at the stop: carry it level, front end slightly raised, and look up for lines and fixtures. Long extension ladders are a two-person carry.

03 · If someone falls

Care first. Then tell your supervisor.

  1. 1Call 911 for a head strike, neck or back pain, unconsciousness, a possible broken bone or heavy bleeding.
  2. 2Don't move them if a head, neck or back injury is possible, unless they're in danger where they are.
  3. 3Make the area safe. Power line involved? Stay back and tell 911.
  4. 4Tell your direct supervisor right away, however minor it seems.
  5. 5Photos and witnesses, the ladder and how it was set. Tag the ladder out.
  6. 6Workplace Accident Report the same day, even if you feel fine (CW-09).
Fell on your own? Check yourself before you get up. If you can't move or are badly hurt, call 911 first.

Near misses count

The ladder slid but you caught it. A foot slipped off a wet rung. Report it. Every near miss is a fall we get to prevent for free.

Customer's property damaged?

Screen, tile, gutter, fixture: tell the customer honestly and tell your supervisor the same day.
