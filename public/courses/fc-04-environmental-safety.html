Environmental Safety

Keeping every drop where the label puts it: out of drains, wells and waterways, off pollinators, pets and the neighbor's garden, and inside the rules of the county you're working in.

Why this course matters

We work on top of the water our customers drink.

Every branch we run sits near the Indian River Lagoon, the St. Lucie River, the Gulf or Central Florida's lakes. What runs off a driveway today is in that water tomorrow.

About 90% of Floridians drink groundwater

Our sandy soils and high water table give pesticide a short trip from the lawn to the aquifer.

The lagoon has already paid

Nutrient runoff fed algae blooms that wiped out seagrass. In 2021 Florida had its deadliest year on record for manatees, driven largely by starvation.

Drift and misuse are illegal

FIFRA and Florida law (s. 487.031) make it unlawful to apply against the label or let pesticide drift onto any person or area not meant to get it. FDACS investigates complaints and can test the property.

02 · Florida's ground and water

Our soil gives pesticide a short trip to the water

High water table

In much of coastal Florida groundwater sits only a few feet below the lawn, closer still in the rainy season. Little soil stands between your spray and the water.

Sandy, fast-draining soil

Sand holds little organic matter to bind pesticide, so water carries it down quickly. Labels warn that products can leach where soils are permeable and groundwater is shallow.

Water everywhere

Canals, retention ponds, seawalls, lakes and the lagoon border our routes. Most storm drains empty straight into them with no treatment at all.

Read the Environmental Hazards box on every outdoor product. Typical wording: "toxic to fish and aquatic invertebrates. Do not apply directly to water."
The label's water setback is the minimum. If the label says "do not apply within 25 feet of water," measure it. A seawall counts as the water's edge.
03 · At the truck

Most water problems start where you park

  1. 1Park away from storm drains, curbs that slope to a drain, ditches and seawalls. Never fill or mix next to a drain or sewer opening.
  2. 2Don't mix on concrete or other hard surfaces where a splash can run to the street. Mix over turf or soil, away from wells and water.
  3. 3Keep an air gap when filling from a hose. Hold the hose above the tank's waterline so mix can't siphon back into the customer's water supply.
  4. 4Never leave a tank filling unattended. Overflow is a spill.
Example label · Environmental hazards

This product is extremely toxic to fish and aquatic invertebrates. Do not apply directly to water, or to areas where surface water is present. Drift and runoff may be hazardous to aquatic organisms in water adjacent to treated areas.

Do not contaminate water when disposing of equipment washwater or rinsate.

Before you leave the branch: spill kit on the truck, every container closed and secured. Any spill: tell your supervisor right away and file a written report the same day. FC-05 covers spill response.
04 · Outdoor perimeter rules

What pyrethroid labels allow around a home

EPA requires runoff-reduction language on outdoor residential pyrethroid labels (bifenthrin, cypermethrin, deltamethrin and others). Check your product: most perimeter sprays carry it.

Foundations

Up to 3 feet up the wall. Soil, turf and vegetation around the structure may be treated per the label.

Hard surfaces

Patios, driveways, sidewalks, porches: spot or crack-and-crevice only. Never broadcast or mist them.

Rain and irrigation

No applications during rain. Don't water the treated area to the point of runoff.

Drains and gutters

Never let product enter or run off into storm drains, ditches, gutters or surface water.

Part two

Drift and weather

Why it matters: drift is the most common environmental complaint against applicators, and the applicator is legally responsible for where the product lands. The weather decides how far it travels.

  2. 02Wind, rain and inversions

01 · How far droplets travel

Smaller droplets stay up longer and land farther away

DropletMist · 100 microns
Time to fall 10 ft10 seconds
Drift in a 3 mph wind44 feet

Illustrative figures of the kind used in university extension training: 10 ft release, steady 3 mph wind. Real drift varies; more wind, height or pressure means farther. For bigger droplets: lower pressure, a coarse setting, nozzle close to the target.

02 · Wind, rain and inversions

Check the sky before you pick up the wand

Wind: a steady 3–10 mph breeze blowing away from sensitive areas is best. Above 10 mph, or gusty, stop. Follow any wind limit on the label.
Rain: never apply in rain. Not sure? Hold a sheet of paper out the window; if it gets wet, it's raining. Hold off when heavy rain is expected before the product dries or sets.
Heat and low humidity shrink droplets and can turn some products to vapor. Midday heat on a dry day raises drift risk. Spray early or late when you can.

Normal: the ground warms the air, it rises and mixes, and fine droplets are diluted and settle nearby.

01 · Pollinators

If it's blooming and bees are on it, don't spray it

Many insecticides we use, including pyrethroids and neonicotinoids, are highly toxic to bees. The label tells you how to protect them.

  • Don't spray flowering plants, hedges or trees while bees are visiting. Treat after petals fall, or target only non-flowering areas.
  • Flowering weeds in turf (clover, Spanish needle) attract bees. Ask the customer to mow before a lawn insecticide when the label calls for it.
  • Keep drift off blooms next to your target.
  • Found a honey bee swarm or hive? Don't treat it on your own. Tell your supervisor, who decides between relocation by a registered beekeeper and treatment.
Example label · Pollinator statements

Environmental hazards. This product is highly toxic to bees and other pollinating insects exposed to direct treatment or residues on blooming plants.

Protect pollinators. Do not apply this product to plants that are flowering. Only apply after all flower petals have fallen off.

Some labels carry a "bee advisory box" with a bee icon. It explains how bees are exposed and how to cut exposure. The restrictions in Directions for Use are legally binding.

02 · Pets, people and wildlife

Clear the area, treat, and keep everyone off until it's dry

Pets

Ask that pets be brought inside. Move or ask the customer to move pet bowls, toys and bedding. Most labels say people and pets stay off treated areas until the spray has dried. Tell the customer how long.

Fish and aquatic life

Pyrethroids kill fish at tiny concentrations. Never spray toward ponds, fountains or aquariums near a lanai. Cover or move bowls and bird baths, and cover koi ponds before treating close by.

Birds and wildlife

Don't spray bird feeders or nests. Use tamper-resistant bait stations, secured and labeled, wherever children, pets or wildlife could reach bait, and as rodenticide labels require.

People first. Nobody, especially children, in the area you're spraying. If someone walks into your spray zone, stop until they're clear.
03 · Sensitive sites

Some places need more care than the label minimum

Read the service notes in PestPac before you start. If a customer or account has a special instruction, it's in there, and it applies every visit.

Not sure an area is safe to treat? Skip it, note it, and call your supervisor. An untreated corner can be fixed tomorrow. A poisoned pond can't.
Schools, daycares and senior communities: treat when occupants are away when you can, follow the account's IPM and notification rules, and keep sprays tight to the target.
Vegetable gardens and fruit trees: no spray unless the product is labeled for that edible crop. Keep drift off them.
Ponds, pools, seawalls and wetlands: keep the label's buffer. Never treat into water.
Customers with health concerns or a neighbor who has asked us to take care: respect the note, and offer the targeted options.

Year-round: no fertilizer on saturated soil, in a storm watch or warning, or before heavy rain.
Combination products count. A granular insecticide with fertilizer in it is fertilizer.
Commercial fertilizing needs a state credential (s. 482.1562). Cities may add their own rules.
02 · Granules and hard surfaces

A granule on the driveway is a granule in the canal

  1. 1Use the deflector shield on rotary spreaders along driveways, sidewalks, beds and water. Several of our counties require it.
  2. 2Sweep or blow every stray granule back onto the turf before you leave. Never into the street, a drain or the water.
  3. 3Grass clippings too. Clippings carry nutrients; ordinances ban blowing them into streets, drains and water.
  4. 4Water in only as the label says. Irrigating to the point of runoff washes product off the lawn.
03 · Rinsate and leftovers

Nothing goes down a drain, onto the ground or into the trash

Leftover mix

Mix only what you need. Use what's left on another site the label allows, at or below the label rate. Never dump it on the ground, in a drain, sink, toilet or ditch.

Rinsate

Rinse water from containers and equipment goes back into the spray tank and is applied to a labeled site. Clean equipment away from drains, wells and water.

Empty containers

Triple rinse or pressure rinse right after emptying, then follow the label's Storage and Disposal section. Never reuse a pesticide container for anything else.

The law: disposing of pesticide or containers against the label, or in a way that pollutes water, is a violation of Florida law (s. 487.031).
Soaked paper towels, absorbent from a concentrate spill, damaged containers: bag them, bring them to the branch and tell your supervisor. FC-05 covers disposal.
04 · Practice · triple rinse

Put the triple rinse in order

Rinse right after you empty a container, before residue dries inside. Select the steps in order.

Wear the PPE the label lists for mixing and loading, including eye protection. Rinse over the tank opening so nothing splashes onto the ground.
