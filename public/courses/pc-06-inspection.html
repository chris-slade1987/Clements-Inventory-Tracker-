Inspection

How a Clements technician walks a property, reads what pests leave behind, finds why they're there, writes it up for the customer and knows when to call in help.

45–60 min5 checkpoints20-question check · 80% to pass

Course objectives

By the end of this course you will be able to:

Explain why inspection comes before treatment, using the access, resources and shelter model
Use your inspection tools (flashlight, mirror, monitors, camera) to see where pests actually live
Follow a systematic route: exterior, roofline, entry points, interior, moisture and conditions
Read signs of activity (droppings, frass, rub marks, shed skins, trails) for species, severity and source
Write findings in PestPac Mobile the customer can understand, with fixes they can make themselves
Recognize when to escalate: termite and WDO signs to your branch manager, rodent entry to Rodent Shield
Why this course matters

Treatment is a part of the service. Inspection is the foundation.

Pests are in a home because they can get in and they can survive there. If you don't find out how, the treatment is temporary, the pests come back, and the customer wonders what they're paying for.

Callbacks start with a skipped inspection

Spraying where pests are seen, not where they live or get in, knocks activity down for a week. The root cause is still there on the next visit.

It's how the law expects us to work

Florida requires every ID cardholder to be trained in IPM (s. 482.091), and IPM starts with inspection and identification. The right product in the right place depends on what you find first.

It's how customers know we're experts

Every truck on the street sprays. The technician who explains why the problem keeps coming back, and how to stop it, is the one customers keep and recommend.

01 · Five steps, outside in

The Clements inspection route

1 · Exterior

Walk the full perimeter: plants, mulch, water, debris, activity.

2 · Entry points

Doors, windows, penetrations, vents, roofline and soffits.

3 · Interior

Kitchen, bathrooms, laundry, garage and attic where in scope.

4 · Moisture and food

Leaks, condensation, grease, pet food, open trash.

5 · Analyze

Access, resources, shelter: what has to change.

Each step informs the next. A shrub on the back wall tells you where to look for a trail inside. Droppings under the sink tell you to check the pipe gap behind them.
Safety on the route: note children, pets, aquariums, edible gardens and pools before you treat (FC-11). Ladders and attics follow FC-06.
02 · Exterior and roofline

Every condition outside is a bridge to the inside

Ground level

Mulch or soil against the foundation or stucco. Shrubs and vines touching the wall. Firewood, pavers, boxes or debris stacked against the house. Burrows along the slab.

Water

Leaky spigots, A/C condensate lines dripping at the slab, sprinklers hitting the wall, low spots that hold water, clogged gutters, buckets, bromeliads and toys holding rain.

Roofline

Tree limbs and palm fronds touching the roof. Gaps at soffits, eaves, roof returns and gable vents. Damaged screens. Grease marks where rats run the edge.

Look for activity too: ant trails on the foundation, droppings, mud tubes on the slab edge, spider webs at eaves, chewed fruit under palms and citrus.
Field note: dense shrubs on the foundation and mulch against the siding are already two recommendations, before you mix a drop of product.
03 · Entry points

Small, overlooked, predictable

  • Gaps around plumbing, electrical and A/C line penetrations
  • Worn, missing or poorly fitted door sweeps; garage door seals
  • Torn screens and gaps around window frames
  • Foundation and stucco cracks, even hairline
  • Dryer vents, exhaust ports, gable and soffit vents
  • Rooflines and anywhere two building materials meet
  • Open weep holes in block or brick
Document every one: where, how big, and what it needs. Rodent Shield documents gaps 1/2 inch or larger (CW-05); mice fit through 1/4 inch, so record those too.

02 · Type, location, volume, distribution

Four questions to ask of every sign

Type

Droppings, shed skins, live or dead pests, damage. Tells you the species and life stage. PC-07 covers identification.

Location

Where harborage and travel routes are. Pests rest close to where they feed.

Volume

How long and how actively it's been going. Old and new droppings together means an established problem.

Distribution

One cabinet is localized. Several rooms is an established, spread-out infestation.

01 · Write it in PestPac Mobile

The customer reads every word

Your notes print on the inspection report emailed to the customer after the visit. Write them for the customer: plain, factual, professional. FC-10 covers the screens.

  1. 1What you found, and where: pest, location, how much
  2. 2Conditions: the access, resources and shelter you saw
  3. 3What you did: treatment and where it went
  4. 4What the customer can do: specific, doable fixes
  5. 5Follow-up: next visit, callback or who will contact them
Photograph it. Attach photos of conditions, entry points and damage to the order. Never photograph people, and never post job photos on social media.
Show it on site. Point the condition out if the customer is home. Where your branch issues Area of Concern flags, use one to mark it.
Office matters go to the office. Anything the office must act on (a follow-up, an estimate request, a hand-off) goes to technotes@clementspestcontrol.com, not in the customer's notes.
Never in notes: blame ("filthy house"), guesses stated as fact ("you have termites"), prices or promises, or card numbers.

03 · Fixes the customer can make

Found it, why it matters, what to do

Many conditions are the customer's to fix. Frame it as problem solving, never blame: what we found, why pests can survive, what we recommend.

"The ants are coming in where this shrub touches the wall, and the drip under your sink gives them water. I've treated where they're traveling. If you can trim the shrub back and get that drip fixed, they lose their way in and their water."
Exclusion, repairs and added services: describe the need; questions about services or pricing go to your branch manager. Never quote a price or promise results.
Common recommendations
Trim plantsso nothing touches the walls or roof
Pull mulch backkeep the foundation edge visible and dry
Fix leaksspigots, sink drains, A/C condensate lines; aim sprinklers off the wall
Seal foodpet food and pantry goods in sealed containers; lids on trash
Reduce cluttercardboard and stored items off the floor and away from walls
Door sweeps and screensreplace worn sweeps and torn screens
Tip and tossempty standing water at least weekly
04 · When to escalate

Some findings are bigger than today's visit

Termite and WDO signs

Mud tubes, swarmers or discarded wings, pellet frass, damaged or hollow wood. Photograph it, leave it intact and tell your branch manager the same day so the termite team follows up. Don't diagnose or promise treatment; WDO inspections and reports need a special WDO ID card or a termite and WDO certified operator.

Rodent entry or activity

Droppings, rub marks, gnawing, attic noise or open entry points. Document and photograph the entry points and present Rodent Shield: inspection and trapping, with sealing on Plus. Seal first, then bait outside; baiting with openings left can leave a rodent dying in a wall.

Beyond our scope

Roof damage, rotted framing, large openings, major plumbing leaks. Note them for the customer and photograph them. They may need a roofer, plumber or contractor before exclusion can work.

Safety hazards

Chewed wiring, sewage leaks, stinging insect nests near doors, unsafe attic access. Stop, tell the customer and your supervisor right away. Don't enter an unsafe space.

Escalate means two things: a customer-facing note of what you observed, and an internal message (branch manager, and technotes@ for the office) so someone owns the follow-up.
