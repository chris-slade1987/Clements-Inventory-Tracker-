Disease & Stress Diagnosis

How to read a lawn that's going brown, find the real cause (water, insects, fungus, chemicals or the site) and explain it so the customer trusts you.

Course objectives

By the end of this course you will be able to:

Recognize the stages of drought stress and confirm it with the screwdriver and footprint tests
Spot common irrigation problems and run a catch-can test to work out run time

Why this course matters

A guess is a callback. A diagnosis is a result.

A brown spot is one of the most common reasons a lawn customer calls upset, and the first thing they often suspect is that we burned it. Many "fungus" calls turn out to be water, insects or chemical injury. The tech who finds the real cause keeps the account.

The wrong product fixes nothing

Fungicide on a chinch bug problem, or insecticide on a watering gap, wastes product and a visit. The lawn keeps declining while the customer keeps paying.

The label is the law

Under FIFRA and Florida Chapter 482 you may only apply a product to a site and pest its label allows. Treating a problem you haven't identified can't meet that standard. FC-02 covers the rules.

Trust is built at the brown spot

Showing a customer exactly what you found, and what will fix it, turns a complaint into a renewal. Getting defensive or guessing does the opposite.

01 · The diagnosis routine

Five questions, then the plant

The symptoms and the conditions have to agree. If the pattern says one thing and the weather says another, keep looking.

Pattern

Walk the whole lawn first. Shape, size, edges, sun or shade, where it sits.

Timing

When did it start? How fast is it moving? What month is it?

Weather

Hot and dry, cool and wet, a rainy stretch, or a cold snap?

Irrigation

Is water reaching this spot? When does the zone run, and for how long?

Recent applications

Check service history: what went down, when, at what rate. Ask about the customer's own products too.

The plant

Pull blades, sheath and roots at the active edge. That's where you confirm.

Ask the customer two things on every brown-spot call: "When did you first notice it?" and "Has anything changed: watering, mowing, a product you put down?" Their answers often point straight at the cause.

03 · Above and below ground

Diagnosis happens with grass in your hand

The blade

Pull blades from the edge of the damage, where it's moving into healthy turf. The center is usually too far gone to read. Look for lesion shape, color and border.

The sheath and runner

Tug a blade. If it slides out of a dark, soft, rotted base while the runner stays firm, think large patch. Black lesions on runners point to take-all.

The roots and soil

Cut a small section with a soil knife. Healthy roots are white and fibrous. Short, black and rotted means take-all. Thin and discolored, not black, fits Pythium root rot. Check soil moisture while you're there.

Carry it on the truck: soil knife or hori-hori, a long screwdriver, a hand lens, an open-ended can for the flotation test (LAWN-07), and your phone camera.
Rule out insects every time. Part the turf at the yellow-to-green margin, or run a flotation test. Chinch bugs, grubs and webworms all mimic disease.

02 · Field tests

Prove it in under a minute

Screwdriver test

Push a long screwdriver into the brown spot, then into green turf nearby. If it stops after an inch or two in the patch but sinks several inches in the green, the root zone isn't getting water.

Footprint test

Walk across the area. Hydrated grass springs back within moments. Drought-stressed blades stay flat, so footprints and tire tracks are still visible minutes later. Look for a blue-gray cast and folded blades too.

Rule out chinch bugs

Chinch damage looks like drought, in the same sunny spots and hot weather. Drought follows the sprinkler pattern and greens up with water. Chinch damage is patchier, spreads outward and doesn't respond to water. Check the margin (LAWN-07).

What dries out first: full sun, slopes, sandy spots, strips along hot driveways and sidewalks, and thin coverage at zone edges. A wedge, half-circle or strip between heads means suspect irrigation first.

01 · The disease calendar

Disease needs a host, a pathogen and the right weather

Typical Florida timing (UF/IFAS). Pythium root rot follows wet soil at any time of year. Fungal pathogens are present all year; weather and water decide when they strike, so take away the wet conditions and you take away the disease.

04 · Fungicide fundamentals

Fungicide stops spread. It doesn't grow grass.

Before you mix, the label must say
  • 1The site: your turf type and residential lawns (some aren't allowed on home lawns)
  • 2The disease you confirmed, by name
  • 3The rate and interval, from the current label and the Clements approved list, never from memory
  • 4Turf tolerance: some DMI fungicides can injure bermudagrass
  • 5Placement: root diseases (take-all, Pythium) are lightly watered in; leaf diseases stay on the leaf

Rotate modes of action

Resistance builds when one group is used over and over; strobilurin-resistant gray leaf spot is already known in the Midwest. Rotate FRAC groups (strobilurins 11, DMIs 3, SDHIs 7) and follow the label's resistance section.

Preventive beats curative

On lawns with a history, apply before the season starts. Take-all programs start about a month before symptoms usually appear. Once a lawn shows symptoms, the cultural fix matters more than the spray.

Calibration and mix rates are covered in LAWN-09.
05 · Ornamental diseases

Shrubs and beds: four you'll see on every route

Powdery mildew

White to gray powdery coating on upper leaves and new growth, often with curling or distortion. Favors shade, crowded plants, humid nights and dry days. Improve airflow and sun; prune for spacing; labeled fungicide if it's severe.

Sooty mold

Black film that wipes off the leaf. It's not attacking the plant: it grows on honeydew from whiteflies, scale, aphids or mealybugs. Look above the black leaves for the insect. Treat the insect, not the mold. It flakes off once the honeydew stops.

Leaf spot

Round or angular spots, often with a dark or yellow border, from fungi or bacteria. Spread by splashing water. Remove and bag fallen leaves, keep irrigation off foliage, and use a labeled product only on plants with a repeat history.

Anthracnose

Irregular tan to dark brown blotches along veins and leaf edges, sometimes twig dieback, after warm wet weather. Prune out infected wood with clean tools, rake up debris, avoid overhead watering.

01 · Chemical and fertilizer burn

Sharp edges and a date on the calendar

Herbicide or chemical injury

Sharp, geometric edges that match a spray path, a drift line or a property boundary. No leaf lesions, no rotted sheath, healthy roots. Blades may look scorched, bleached or twisted.

  • Some herbicides injure St. Augustine and centipede, and many labels limit use above certain temperatures. Read the label (LAWN-06).
  • Check for the customer's own weed-and-feed, a neighbor's spray, gas or oil spills, or pool water.

Fertilizer burn

Stripes or spots that line up with spreader passes, overlaps at turns, or a spill where a bag was set down or a hopper was filled. Shows within days of an application.

  • Worse on drought-stressed turf, wet foliage, and with quick-release nitrogen in heat.
  • Fertilizer timing and summer restrictions are covered in LAWN-05.

02 · Environmental stress

Sometimes the site is the problem

Shade

Thin, spindly turf under trees and on north sides. St. Augustine and zoysia cope best but still need some direct sun. Raise the mowing height, cut back nitrogen and water. Beds or groundcover may be the honest answer.

Compaction

Thin, weak turf along paths, parking spots and play areas. The screwdriver won't go in even when soil is moist. Water runs off. Recommend core aeration and redirecting traffic.

Mowing too low

Scalped, brown, stemmy turf after mowing, worst on high spots. Low mowing invites weeds, insects, take-all and drought stress. Never remove more than one-third of the blade at a time.

Salt

Browning on the ocean or road side after wind events, storm surge, or salty well water. Tip burn and white crust on soil. Seashore paspalum tolerates salt best; centipede and bahia poorly. Heavy fresh water flushes salt.

05 · Close the loop

A diagnosis nobody wrote down didn't happen

  • 1PestPac Mobile notes print on the customer's report: what you found, applied and recommend, in plain, blame-free words.
  • 2Office action (a follow-up visit, an irrigation referral note, possible company-caused damage) goes to technotes@clementspestcontrol.com.
  • 3Recheck it first next visit. Did it respond, spread or change? Confirm or revise the diagnosis. That's how you get sharper.
  • 4Set the timeline honestly. Drought: green-up in 1 to 2 weeks once water is fixed. Disease: it stops spreading first, then fills in as the grass grows.

When you're not sure

Say "let me confirm before I treat." Photograph the blade, sheath and roots, and call a senior tech or your branch manager. For something unusual, a sample can go through the county Extension office to the UF/IFAS Plant Diagnostic Center.

Never call a product "safe" or promise a result. If the customer asks, give the product name, active ingredient and label precautions (482.2265).
